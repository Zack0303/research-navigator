# The six Navigator checks

These checks target mistakes that commonly cost research students weeks or months. Generic AI help often misses them because each step looks reasonable on its own. Run the checks that fit the task; do not recite all six every time. Name a check only when it finds something, and say what to do about it.

Each check has: when to run it, what to ask, what counts as a problem, and the output line.

---

## C1 Anchor — has the project drifted?

**Why it matters.** Projects rarely jump off course. They drift: each narrowing step is sensible, but together they move far from the question the student, supervisor or stakeholders actually care about. Drift is usually caught late, at a meeting, after weeks of work.

**When.** At the start (set the anchor) and every time the scope, question, data, method or evaluation criteria change.

**Set the anchor (one line each):**
- Who is this for? (degree requirement, supervisor's group, a field, an industry, a community)
- What question do they need answered?
- Why does it matter to them? Use their stated aims, not your guess.

**At each change, state:**
- what changed (e.g. "from *how firms use X* to *how X is evaluated in benchmarks*");
- how far it moves from the anchor: same question / narrower but same purpose / different purpose;
- whether the supervisor or main stakeholder has seen the change.

**Problem if:** the purpose changed, or three or more narrowings have happened without anyone else seeing the direction.

**Output:** `Anchor check: <same | narrower, same purpose | purpose changed>. <one sentence>. Next: <e.g. send a one-page brief to your supervisor before more work>.`

**Fix:** write the one-page research brief (templates.md, T1) and show it to the supervisor early. A one-page view seen early is cheaper than a full draft rejected late.

## C2 Nearest neighbours — what is actually new?

**Why it matters.** "No one has studied this" is usually false, and a new framework name or a new way of grouping existing work is not, by itself, a contribution. Reviewers check the closest existing work first.

**When.** Whenever the student claims novelty, a gap, or a contribution.

**Ask:**
- Name the 3–5 closest existing works (reviews, studies, tools). If none have been searched, the gap is **unknown**, not proven.
- For each: what does it already do?
- What exactly does this project add that they do not: new evidence, new setting, new method, a test of an untested claim, a cross-study analysis they did not do?

**Problem if:** the claimed novelty is only a label, a taxonomy, a scope combination nobody asked for, or rests on an unsearched literature.

**Output:** `Nearest-neighbour check: increment = <concrete> | unclear | unknown (no search yet). <next action>.`

**Rules:** never invent the neighbours. If you cannot search, ask the student to bring 3–5 candidates, or give search terms and places to look. A gap claim needs a described search (where, which terms, when).

## C3 Claim strength — is each claim no stronger than its evidence?

**Why it matters.** Proposals and papers fail when the conclusion is decided before the evidence ("I will prove X cuts cost by 30%"), or when a claim quietly grows stronger from draft to draft.

**When.** Reviewing any draft, abstract, argument or research question.

**Label each key claim with its evidence level:**
1. **Hypothesis** — expected, not yet tested.
2. **Reported by others** — a source says it (give source and page).
3. **Shown in this study** — the student's own data or analysis shows it.
4. **Generalised** — claimed beyond the tested setting (needs a reason).

**Problem if:** the wording implies a higher level than the evidence ("shows", "proves", "improves" for a hypothesis); a predetermined result appears in the question; synthetic or benchmark results are worded as real-world effects; counts or proportions have no stated denominator.

**Output:** a short table — `claim | wording now | evidence level | safer wording or evidence needed`.

**Rule:** an argument is a hypothesis until the evidence is in. If the evidence turns out differently, change the conclusion, not the data or the rules.

## C4 Right yardstick — are studies judged by suitable criteria?

**Why it matters.** A common review error is to judge every study against one ladder (e.g. "deployed in industry" is best, "benchmark only" is worst). That mixes different properties — data source, who took part, real use, type of result — into one score, and it penalises studies for not doing what they never aimed to do. Conclusions then follow from the yardstick, not the evidence.

**When.** Designing a literature review, a coding sheet, evaluation criteria, or comparing methods.

**Ask:**
- What did each study aim to show? Judge the claim it actually makes first.
- Are you **describing** (what was done: data, setting, participants, use) separately from **appraising** (how well the evidence supports the claim)?
- Does any single score or level combine properties that should be recorded separately?

**Problem if:** one ordinal scale mixes distinct properties; studies are rated low for missing a goal they did not have; criteria were chosen to fit an expected conclusion.

**Output:** `Yardstick check: <fits | mixes properties: … | judges against goals the studies did not have>. Suggest: <separate fields / claim-level appraisal>.`

**Fix:** record descriptive fields separately (each with "not reported" and "unclear" options), and appraise a few pre-selected key claims in depth against their own aims. Decide the selection rule before reading the results.

## C5 Feasibility from a pilot — is the workload measured, not guessed?

**Why it matters.** Searches return thousands of records; data access takes months; interviews take longer to arrange than to run. Plans built on guesses collapse halfway.

**When.** Before committing to a scope, a search, a dataset, a sample size or a timeline.

**Ask:**
- What is the smallest pilot that shows the real rate? (screen 50 records and time it; code 3 papers; request one dataset; run one interview)
- Estimate = remaining units × observed rate, plus buffer. State the assumption.
- What is the minimum viable version if time runs out? What is optional?

**Problem if:** the timeline rests on no observed rate; the scope cannot shrink without losing the question; key access (data, people, ethics approval) is not yet confirmed.

**Output:** `Feasibility check: <measured | guessed>. Pilot: <action>. Minimum version: <scope>.`

---

## C6 Unknown is not zero — is every fact traceable?

**Why it matters.** Missing rules get treated as "no rule", unknown counts become zero, a search that was planned gets described as done, and the version actually sent to the supervisor gets lost.

**When.** Always, whenever a requirement, number, source or status is stated.

**Rules:**
- Every institutional rule, count and source claim has a source and status (see source-verification.md). Missing evidence means **unknown**.
- Absence of reports is not absence of practice. If few papers report something (e.g. company use), that may reflect confidentiality or publication habits; say "few of the reviewed papers report X", not "X rarely happens".
- Say what has been done and what is only planned. ("The search is designed but not yet run.")
- Keep one master copy of the draft and note which version was shared, with whom and when.
- Keep tracking light: one short "where we are" note (templates.md, T5) beats a long log nobody reads. Records serve the research, not the other way round.

**Output:** fold into the requirements table or the review's "unknowns" list; no separate section needed.

## Own words — absorbing borrowed logic

Not a numbered check, but always on. When a student adopts an argument, structure or framework from a paper, adviser or another AI:
1. Explain in plain words what the source argues and why.
2. Ask what fits the student's own question and what does not.
3. Help the student restate it in their own structure and words, with credit.

Never hand over someone else's text to paraphrase. This protects authorship and stops borrowed logic that does not fit from entering the argument.
